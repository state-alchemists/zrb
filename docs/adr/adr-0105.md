# ADR-0105 — Barge-in cancels zrb's echo in process, with a pluggable canceller lined up by measuring the delay

**Status:** Accepted

**Context.** Barge-in (ADR-0076) lets the user talk over zrb. It first shipped as a `headset` mode that trusted the microphone not to hear zrb. On a laptop's own speakers the microphone does hear zrb, and a user who turned it on there saw zrb transcribe the end of its own reply ("22 uncommitted changes") and answer it. Reproduced on a MacBook: with the microphone trusted, three of zrb's sentences came back as utterances; with barge-in off, none did.

Telling the user from zrb's echo by the words heard (zrb knows what it is saying) was measured first and rejected: loud echo in a room is transcribed as *other* words ("doctor", "jeffrey"), which look exactly like someone else talking, so it interrupted itself on every loud sentence. Echo cancellation — subtracting what zrb played from what the microphone heard — was prototyped instead: a NumPy partitioned-block adaptive filter against WebRTC's canceller, first on a simulated laptop, then on a real MacBook recording. Both left vosk one stray word in 13 seconds of zrb talking, down from all 41.

The real-device runs failed four times before that, each for a timing reason, and those shaped the design as much as the algorithm:

- PortAudio reported 705 ms of input latency on a device whose echo delay was 49 ms; trusting it put the reference 0.65 s off, and nothing was removed.
- Microphone blocks stamped with the callback's clock wobble by milliseconds, which the filter takes for a room that keeps changing.
- A reference 40 ms *behind* the echo removed nothing even for WebRTC: an echo that arrives before its reference cannot be subtracted.
- Each utterance opens a new output stream whose start drifts from its reported latency by tens of milliseconds, so the measured delay jumped by 60 ms between sentences, and every jump restarted the canceller.

**Decision.** With `barge_in=on`, dictation removes zrb's voice from every microphone block before measuring it.

1. **zrb plays its speech itself.** Speech that a backend can render is played through sounddevice (ADR-0103), and every chunk is written to `echo_reference` (16 kHz, a ring of the last ten seconds) at the time the host says it reaches the speakers (`outputBufferDacTime`). Speech a player program plays leaves nothing to cancel with, so its echo stays unheard (barge-in behaves as off for it) — Termux's own voice, or `player=command`.
2. **The canceller is a backend.** `AnyEchoCanceller.process(mic, far)` takes each 16 kHz microphone block with the reference lined up to it; `is_converged`, `needs_reference` and `reset` complete it. Built-ins: `numpy` (default), a partitioned-block frequency-domain adaptive filter (the Speex "MDF" algorithm) with a double-talk guard and a residual echo suppressor, in pure NumPy so it runs wherever dictation does, Termux included; and `none`, which trusts the microphone — headphones, or the system's own echo cancellation (PipeWire/PulseAudio echo-cancel).
3. **`EchoCancellation` lines the two up.** Microphone blocks are stamped with the host's ADC time (`inputBufferAdcTime`), else counted in samples. The echo delay is measured from the audio: every second, GCC-PHAT over the last two seconds of microphone against what was played, across −0.5 to +1 s; two agreeing estimates lock it. The reference is read at that delay minus a 40 ms lead, so it always leads the echo; the filter covers 320 ms. Once locked, only a jump over 25 ms (another output device) re-locks and resets the canceller; drift is left to the filter.
4. **The microphone stays deaf over zrb until cancellation is ready.** Ready means the delay is locked, the canceller has adapted over 2 s of zrb speaking, and in the last 20 blocks of zrb speaking at most one was left at or over `threshold` — what is left of zrb's voice cannot start an utterance. Once ready it stays ready, since the user talking over zrb is loud on purpose, until a re-lock. One `EchoCancellation` per session keeps what it learned across microphone reopens.
5. **A barge-in pauses first** (ADR-0076): a stray leftover that does start an utterance costs a moment's pause and is resumed, not a reply cut off.

**Consequences.** Barge-in works on laptop speakers with no native library. On a MacBook's own speakers and microphone the delay locked at 46 ms and held within half a millisecond across five sentences, cancellation was ready 6.5 s after zrb started speaking, zrb's voice never triggered barge-in, and a second voice talking over it was heard and transcribed. The cost is a warm-up of a few seconds of zrb speaking once a session, during which the user cannot talk over it; about 1.5 % of a core for the canceller; and in-process playback for speech. The NumPy canceller is weaker than WebRTC's when speakers distort at high volume; a WebRTC canceller can be added as another backend (livekit ships one) without changing anything else.

**Alternatives rejected.**

| Alternative | Why rejected |
| --- | --- |
| Trust the microphone (`headset` mode) | On speakers zrb hears its own voice and answers it. Kept as the `none` canceller, chosen on purpose. |
| Tell echo from the user by the words heard | Loud room echo is transcribed as other words, so zrb interrupted itself on 4 of 4 loud sentences. |
| Trust the audio driver's reported latency | Off by 0.65 s on a MacBook; the delay has to be measured. |
| Stamp blocks with the callback's clock | Jitter of milliseconds per block reads as a changing room; the host's DAC/ADC times do not drift. |
| WebRTC's canceller as the default | Needs a native wheel (none for Windows on ARM, none on Termux), and matched the NumPy canceller on the real recording. |
| Re-lock on every measured change | Clock drift and estimate wobble reset the canceller every few seconds, and it never became ready. |
| Judge convergence by dB of echo removed | On a laptop the echo is a few dB above the microphone's noise, so the bar is never reached; noise measured between sentences also includes their echo tail. What matters is whether what is left can start an utterance. |
